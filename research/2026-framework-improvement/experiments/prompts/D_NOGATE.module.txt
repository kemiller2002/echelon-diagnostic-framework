Method: apply the following diagnostic method (experimental EDF variant) internally, then answer the scored items.

1. Treat the presenting problem as one hypothesis. Restate the client's framing as a hypothesis, not a fact. Before accepting it, consider rivals that are easy to miss: there is no real problem (normal variation, baseline or peer comparison explains it); the pattern is an intentional, accepted tradeoff; the cause is external to the organization; the signal is a measurement artifact; the cause lies in a different unit than the one blamed.

2. State the outcome factually and check the measure. Describe what actually changed, against a baseline or comparison. For every metric relied on, ask how it is produced, who controls it, whether its definition or collection changed, and whether people are rewarded on it (rewarded metrics can be gamed and can hide deterioration).

3. Keep an evidence ledger. For each item note who produced it, whether that source has a stake in the conclusion, and whether it is independent (system records, external data, audits, direct observation) or self-report. Treat claims supported only by interested parties as unestablished until corroborated. No stakeholder group is automatically right. Silence is not health: when reporting is penalized or participation drops, absence of reports is not evidence of absence. Missing evidence makes a claim unknown, never contradicted.

4. Build the origin network. Allow several interacting causes, feedback loops, delays, and causes that sit outside the unit that shows the symptom. Separate the mechanism that creates the problem from controls that merely reveal or contain it.

5. Grade every causal link. Causal only when the evidence shows temporal order, a mechanism, covariation, and the main rival ruled out, or an intervention or natural experiment shows the effect. Co-occurrence without that is association only. A contrast case where the supposed cause is present without the effect, or the effect occurs without the cause, counts against the link.

6. Identify control points where intervention could influence future outcomes, and rank them by influence, controllability, cost and confidence.

7. Determine diagnostic sufficiency: do we understand enough to take the next responsible action?

8. Note unknowns and the next responsible action.
