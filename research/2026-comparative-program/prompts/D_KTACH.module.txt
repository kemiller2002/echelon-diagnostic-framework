Method: combine Kepner-Tregoe Problem Analysis with Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Heuer). Use Problem Analysis to specify the problem and generate causes; use Analysis of Competing Hypotheses to weigh them; use Decision Analysis for actions.

Part 1 - Problem Analysis (Kepner-Tregoe).
1. State the deviation precisely: what should be happening, what is actually happening, and the gap, in measurable terms. A complaint or opinion is not a deviation until it is specified.
2. Specify the problem along four dimensions, each as IS versus IS NOT:
   - WHAT: which object, unit, product or group shows the deviation, and which comparable ones that could but do not?
   - WHERE: in which locations, processes, teams or channels is it observed, and where is it not?
   - WHEN: when was it first observed, when since, under what conditions or cycle, and when not?
   - EXTENT: how many, how much, what trend, what share - and what is not affected?
3. For each IS/IS NOT pair, list distinctions: what is true of the IS that is not true of the IS NOT.
4. For each distinction, list changes: what changed in, on, around or about that distinction, and when.
5. Develop possible causes from distinctions and changes. A cause must explain how the change produced the deviation.
6. Test each possible cause against every IS/IS NOT pair: does it explain both why the deviation IS and why it IS NOT? Note assumptions required for it to fit. The most probable cause explains the specification with the fewest and most reasonable assumptions.
7. Verify the most probable cause: identify the cheapest observation or test that would confirm it before acting. Until verified, it remains a probable cause.

Part 2 - Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Heuer).
1. List every reasonable hypothesis, including ones you think unlikely, that the evidence could support. Include "no real problem" and "an outside cause" where plausible.
2. List the significant evidence and assumptions, including the absence of things you would expect to see.
3. Build a matrix: for each item of evidence, judge whether it is consistent, inconsistent, or not applicable to each hypothesis. Note which items are diagnostic, meaning consistent with some hypotheses and inconsistent with others. Evidence consistent with every hypothesis has no diagnostic value.
4. Refine the matrix: merge, split or add hypotheses as needed; drop evidence with no diagnostic value from the weighing.
5. Draw tentative conclusions by trying to disprove hypotheses. The most likely hypothesis is the one with the least inconsistent evidence, not the one with the most consistent evidence.
6. Test sensitivity: identify the few items of evidence that drive the conclusion, and ask whether any could be wrong, misinterpreted, gamed, or deceptive. Consider who produced each and what they had at stake.
7. Report the relative likelihood of all hypotheses, not only the leading one.
8. Identify the indicators or evidence that would change the conclusion, and prefer collecting evidence that discriminates between the leading hypotheses.

Part 3 - Decision Analysis (Kepner-Tregoe) for actions.
1. State the decision purpose.
2. Separate MUST objectives (mandatory, measurable, realistic) from WANT objectives (weighted by importance).
3. Screen alternatives against MUSTs; score surviving alternatives against WANTs.
4. Perform adverse-consequence analysis for leading alternatives: probability and seriousness of what could go wrong, including effects on other parts of the system and on information flow.
5. Prefer the alternative with the best balance of performance against WANTs and acceptable risk. If the cause is not yet verified, prefer verification steps and reversible actions over commitments that assume the cause.

Part 4 - Potential Problem Analysis.
For the chosen path, identify what could go wrong, likely causes, preventive actions, and contingent actions with triggers.

Apply this method internally, then answer the scored items.
