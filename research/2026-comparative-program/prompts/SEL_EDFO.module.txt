Method: use EDF as the diagnostic record and choose techniques step by step (experimental orchestration variant).

1. Keep an EDF diagnostic record throughout the analysis: System Context (primary system, context, focus); Outcome stated factually; Manifestations; an evidence ledger (for each item: who produced it, whether that source has a stake, whether it is independent, and whether it is observation or inference); the live hypotheses; the Origin Network; Control Points; and Diagnostic Sufficiency.
2. At each step, choose the technique from the reference below that fits the question you face now:
   - Localizing a deviation (what, where, when and how much is affected, and what comparable things are not): Kepner-Tregoe specification and distinctions.
   - Several live explanations: Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (weigh diagnostic evidence; prefer the hypothesis with the least inconsistent evidence).
   - Disputed, interested or possibly gamed sources: the evidence-ledger provenance check.
   - A simple, well-evidenced problem: stop structuring and answer directly.
3. When new evidence changes the question, switch technique and keep the record. Do not restart.
4. Report the techniques you used, in order, in the output field methodChoice.


## Additional decision rules for this analysis

1. Treat the presenting request as one hypothesis, not a fact. Keep its rivals live until evidence rules them out: no real problem (normal variation or a baseline explains it); an intentional tradeoff; an outside cause; a measurement or reporting artifact; a cause in a different unit or component from the one blamed.
2. Judge every action by what it would do under every live hypothesis, not only the leading one. An action is harmful if it is likely to cause damage even when the leading hypothesis is true: punishing the messenger or the wrong party, suppressing information, rewarding a gamed metric, or disabling a protective control. An action is premature if it is only sensible when a still-unconfirmed hypothesis is true.
3. "No intervention is warranted" is a valid, sufficient conclusion when the evidence shows the problem does not exist or is an accepted tradeoff.
4. Evidence collection is governed by value of information. Before each collection, name the live hypotheses and the answer or action that is most uncertain. Collect an item only if its title suggests it could discriminate between live hypotheses or change your next action. Stop as soon as no remaining item could change your next action. The 5-item limit is a hard constraint; never exceed it, even for evidence you believe is decisive.

# EDF Specification v0.3

Status: Working draft

This is the authoritative EDF v0.3 specification for the current draft.

Under EDF's constitutional version integrity rule, this defines a subsequent version rather than changing the meaning of v0.2 retroactively.

EDF evolves under constitutional rules defined in [constitution.md](constitution.md). The constitution governs framework evolution, not how to perform an EDF analysis.

## Purpose

EDF is a systems understanding framework with a diagnostic grammar.

It is used to explain how a system produced an outcome and to identify the highest-leverage opportunities for influencing future outcomes.

EDF is not primarily a decision framework. It establishes confidence in reality before decision frameworks such as Clarity evaluate what to do next.

Diagnostic Calibration is an optional extension for comparing, challenging, refining, and synthesizing multiple EDF analyses of the same system.

Framework changes should remain consistent with the constitutional rules of EDF.

## Design Rule

If a new concept does not improve one of the five verbs, it does not belong in EDF.

The five verbs are:

- Define
- Observe
- Explain
- Influence
- Validate

## Context Principle

Every EDF analysis is valid only within its declared System Context.

## Required Diagnostic Sequence

Every EDF analysis should follow this sequence:

1. Define the System Context
   - Primary System
   - Context
   - Focus
2. Define the Outcome
3. Identify Manifestation(s)
4. Build the Origin Network
5. Trace Propagation
6. Evaluate Evidence
7. Identify Control Points
8. Rank Control Points
9. Determine Diagnostic Sufficiency

## Sequence Notes

### 1. Define the System Context

Every EDF begins by declaring its System Context.

System Context has three required fields:

- Primary System
- Context
- Focus

These define what is being analyzed, what larger system materially influences it, and what aspect of the primary system is under examination.

Different analyses may disagree partly because they declared different System Contexts. That is not automatically a conflict in evidence.

### 2. Define the Outcome

State the outcome in factual terms, not interpretive terms.

Good:

- Two aircraft were lost and the fleet was grounded
- The mission landed on the moon and returned safely

Weak:

- Management failed
- The launch culture was broken

### 3. Identify Manifestation(s)

Manifestations are observable signals.

They are how the outcome appears in the world. A complex outcome may have several manifestations.

### 4. Build the Origin Network

Identify the contributing origins that made the outcome possible.

Origins may be:

- Technical
- Human
- Organizational
- Environmental
- Financial
- Regulatory

Complex outcomes should usually be modeled as an origin network rather than a single root cause.

### 5. Trace Propagation

Describe how effects moved from the origin network to the manifestations and outcome.

Propagation paths may be:

- Physical
- Informational
- Organizational
- Financial
- Human

### 6. Evaluate Evidence

Separate direct observation from inference.

Evaluate the quality of evidence supporting the current model and note meaningful gaps or disputes.

### 7. Identify Control Points

Identify where intervention could most effectively influence future outcomes.

Control points may differ from origins.

### 8. Rank Control Points

Rank candidate control points using:

- Influence
- Controllability
- Cost
- Confidence

The ranking does not need false precision. It does need a defensible ordering.

### 9. Determine Diagnostic Sufficiency

Ask:

> Do we understand enough to take the next responsible action?

Diagnosis is sufficient when additional investigation is unlikely to materially change the next responsible action.

## Required Output Elements

All EDF outputs should include, at minimum:

- System Context
- Primary System
- Context
- Focus
- Outcome
- Manifestation(s)
- Origin Network
- Propagation
- Evidence
- Control Points
- Control Point Ranking
- Confidence
- Diagnostic Sufficiency
- Unknowns
- Next Responsible Action

## Optional Extension: Diagnostic Calibration

Diagnostic Calibration is not required for routine EDF use.

Use it when:

- The situation is complex
- The stakes are high
- Causes are disputed
- Multiple disciplines are involved
- The cost of being wrong is significant

Diagnostic Calibration uses multiple independent EDF analyses to improve confidence in the diagnostic model while preserving valuable disciplinary perspectives.

See [diagnostic-calibration.md](diagnostic-calibration.md).

## Relationship To Complexity Levels

- Use [EDF-0](complexity-levels.md#edf-0-rapid) for rapid, low-complexity situations.
- Use [EDF-1](complexity-levels.md#edf-1-standard) when evidence and alternatives matter.
- Use [EDF-2](complexity-levels.md#edf-2-complex) when multiple interacting origins and layered control points must be modeled.

The sequence remains the same across levels. The depth changes.

## Relationship To Clarity

EDF creates the diagnostic grammar.

Diagnostic Calibration improves confidence in the diagnostic model.

Clarity uses the calibrated understanding to evaluate decisions.


=== Technique reference C: Kepner-Tregoe ===
Method: apply Kepner-Tregoe Problem Analysis and Decision Analysis.

Part 1 - Problem Analysis (Kepner-Tregoe).
1. State the deviation precisely: what should be happening, what is actually happening, and the gap, in measurable terms. A complaint or opinion is not a deviation until it is specified.
2. Specify the problem along four dimensions, each as IS versus IS NOT:
   - WHAT: which object, unit, product or group shows the deviation, and which comparable ones that could but do not?
   - WHERE: in which locations, processes, teams or channels is it observed, and where is it not?
   - WHEN: when was it first observed, when since, under what conditions or cycle, and when not?
   - EXTENT: how many, how much, what trend, what share - and what is not affected?
3. For each IS/IS NOT pair, list distinctions: what is true of the IS that is not true of the IS NOT.
4. For each distinction, list changes: what changed in, on, around or about that distinction, and when.
5. Develop possible causes from distinctions and changes. A cause must explain how the change produced the deviation.
6. Test each possible cause against every IS/IS NOT pair: does it explain both why the deviation IS and why it IS NOT? Note assumptions required for it to fit. The most probable cause explains the specification with the fewest and most reasonable assumptions.
7. Verify the most probable cause: identify the cheapest observation or test that would confirm it before acting. Until verified, it remains a probable cause.

Part 2 - Decision Analysis (Kepner-Tregoe) for actions.
1. State the decision purpose.
2. Separate MUST objectives (mandatory, measurable, realistic) from WANT objectives (weighted by importance).
3. Screen alternatives against MUSTs; score surviving alternatives against WANTs.
4. Perform adverse-consequence analysis for leading alternatives: probability and seriousness of what could go wrong, including effects on other parts of the system and on information flow.
5. Prefer the alternative with the best balance of performance against WANTs and acceptable risk. If the cause is not yet verified, prefer verification steps and reversible actions over commitments that assume the cause.

Part 3 - Potential Problem Analysis.
For the chosen path, identify what could go wrong, likely causes, preventive actions, and contingent actions with triggers.

Apply this method internally, then answer the scored items.


=== Technique reference D: Analysis of Competing Hypotheses ===
Part 2 - Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Heuer).
1. List every reasonable hypothesis, including ones you think unlikely, that the evidence could support. Include "no real problem" and "an outside cause" where plausible.
2. List the significant evidence and assumptions, including the absence of things you would expect to see.
3. Build a matrix: for each item of evidence, judge whether it is consistent, inconsistent, or not applicable to each hypothesis. Note which items are diagnostic, meaning consistent with some hypotheses and inconsistent with others. Evidence consistent with every hypothesis has no diagnostic value.
4. Refine the matrix: merge, split or add hypotheses as needed; drop evidence with no diagnostic value from the weighing.
5. Draw tentative conclusions by trying to disprove hypotheses. The most likely hypothesis is the one with the least inconsistent evidence, not the one with the most consistent evidence.
6. Test sensitivity: identify the few items of evidence that drive the conclusion, and ask whether any could be wrong, misinterpreted, gamed, or deceptive. Consider who produced each and what they had at stake.
7. Report the relative likelihood of all hypotheses, not only the leading one.
8. Identify the indicators or evidence that would change the conclusion, and prefer collecting evidence that discriminates between the leading hypotheses.
